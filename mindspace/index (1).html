History

    What is stress?

    Stress is the body's response to a challenge or demand. In the short term it can be helpful: it can sharpen attention and make energy available. When stress is repeated or lasts a long time and there is not enough recovery, it may contribute to strain. Experiences differ between people, and harmful effects are not inevitable.

    Meet the brain

    Amygdala

    Helps process emotionally important information, including possible threats, and contributes to stress responses.

    Hippocampus

    Important for learning, memory and context. It is also involved in helping regulate stress-related processes.

    Prefrontal cortex

    Supports attention, planning, decision-making and regulating our responses.

    No single brain region controls all emotions or behaviour. These areas work together in networks.

    The fast stress response

    The sympathetic nervous system helps prepare the body to respond to a challenge. Hormones such as adrenaline and noradrenaline are involved, and people may notice changes in alertness, heart rate and energy availability. Responses vary from person to person.

    1. Challenge or perceived threat
    2. Nervous system responds
    3. Sympathetic activation
    4. Body prepares to respond

    The HPA axis

    A slower hormone pathway links the brain and the adrenal glands.

    1. Hypothalamusreleases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone)
    2. CRH
    3. Pituitary glandresponds by releasing ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone)
    4. ACTH
    5. Adrenal cortexresponds by releasing cortisol
    6. Cortisol

    Cortisol helps coordinate the body's response to stress and regulates many processes. The HPA axis also uses feedback regulation, so it is more complex than a one-way chain.

    Short-term vs prolonged stress

    Short-term stress

    • Can help mobilise attention and energy
    • A normal biological response
    • Usually helps the body adapt to a challenge

    Repeated or prolonged stress

    • May contribute to sleep difficulties
    • May affect concentration and memory
    • May influence mood and emotional regulation
    • May affect metabolic and immune regulation

    These outcomes vary and do not happen to everyone.

    Recovery and resilience

    Recovery involves biological, behavioural and social factors. Things that can help include:

    • Enough sleep
    • Regular physical activity
    • Study breaks and relaxation
    • Social connection
    • Support from trusted people
    • Professional support when needed

    Habits do not cure mental health conditions. Support from a professional matters.
